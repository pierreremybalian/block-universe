A photograph of “the world right now” is a slice through the block. Which slice depends on how you are moving, so two observers at the same place and moment take two different photographs of the same events. The difference grows with distance: a tilted slice meets far-off things earlier on one side and later on the other.

The rotor makes the tilt visible because it changes quickly. In B’s slice the left side of the rotor is caught at an earlier moment than the right side, so the blades, straight at every instant, come out curved. Nothing about the rotor is bent; only the slice is tilted.

A real camera records something different again. It collects light that arrives at the same moment, which left nearer objects later and farther objects earlier. That is a cut along the past light cone, not along a now. Terrell showed in 1959 that this makes fast-moving objects look rotated rather than squashed. The light-cone photograph is the one the whole sky gives us, and it comes back in the last stage.
