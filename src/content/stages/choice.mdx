If every moment is as real as every other, then there is already a fact about what you will do tomorrow, in the same way there is a fact about what you did yesterday. Rietdijk titled his 1966 paper “A Rigorous Proof of Determinism Derived from the Special Theory of Relativity.” Putnam ended his 1967 paper more bluntly: “I conclude that the problem of the reality and the determinateness of future events is now solved. Moreover, it is solved by physics and not by philosophy.”

Four ideas tend to get mixed up here. The block universe is *eternalism*: the future exists. It is not *determinism*, which says the laws plus the state of the world at one time fix every other time. A block could contain truly random events; it would simply contain the outcomes that happen. It is not *fatalism* either, which says what will happen will happen whatever you do. And it is not *predestination*, which says the future was settled beforehand. Nothing comes “before” the block: your future is fixed at the times it happens, the way your past is, not in advance of them.

The ancient version of the worry was the “lazy argument,” reported by Cicero: if you are fated to recover from an illness, you will recover whether or not you call a doctor, so why call one? The Stoic Chrysippus replied that recovering and calling the doctor are fated together. The outcome comes through the action, not around it. The block says the same. Your deliberation and your decision are in it, and what lies in their future light cone is the way it is partly because of them.

Why does the future still feel open? On a view argued by David Albert and others, both everyday asymmetries come from the same source as the arrow of stage 10: entropy was low in the past. Records, memories included, form only toward the low-entropy end, so you can know the past. Your actions spread out toward the high-entropy end, so you can affect the future. On this account the open future is a fact about what you can know and what you can change, not about what exists.

Penrose’s Andromedans fit here too. Their decision to launch a fleet lies in your elsewhere, outside both your cones: you can neither learn it nor affect it. That is why relativity can disagree about whether it has already been made, for two walkers in a street, without either of them being able to tell.

What remains open is whether the block is right. On a growing block, or George Ellis’s crystallizing block, the future comes into being as it happens; in many-worlds quantum mechanics, every branch exists. Whether free will needs an open future at all is an older debate. Compatibilists say it doesn’t: what matters is that your choices come from your own reasons. Libertarians about free will say it does.
