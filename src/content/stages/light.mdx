Every event has two cones attached to it. The future cone is where its light, or anything slower, can reach. The past cone is everything that could have reached it. Because nothing travels faster than light, these cones mark the edge of cause and effect.

Everything outside both cones is called elsewhere. Those events are too far away in space, for the time between them, for any signal to connect them in either direction. Nothing that happens there can make any difference here, and nothing here can make a difference there.

The cones are drawn at 45 degrees because the block uses matched units: one light-hour across for every hour up. What makes them special is that every observer agrees on them. Tilt the frame by any speed and worldlines lean, the grid skews, but each cone keeps exactly its shape. That single fact, measured again and again since 1887, is what forces the rest of relativity, including the next surprise: observers who agree about light cannot agree about which distant events are happening now.
